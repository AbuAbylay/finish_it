# PMP exam prep: study notes

Sample course for Finish-it coach. Original study notes written for this repository. They are not official PMI material and are not copied from any publication. The notes cover nine core topics that a candidate meets early when preparing for the Project Management Professional exam.

## What a project is

A project is a temporary effort undertaken to create a unique product, service, or result. Temporary means the work has a defined beginning and a defined end; it does not mean the work is short, and some projects run for years. Unique means the outcome differs in some important way from anything the organization has produced before, even when parts of the work feel familiar. A project ends when its objectives are met, when it is stopped because the objectives cannot be met, or when the need for it no longer exists.

Operations are the opposite kind of work. They are ongoing and repetitive, and they keep an organization running day after day: processing payroll every month, answering support calls, restocking shelves. Operations have no planned end date, and success means doing the same thing reliably at a steady cost. The two kinds of work meet at handover. Building a new payroll system is a project; running payroll with that system afterwards is operations. A project manager plans for this handover from the start, because a result that nobody is ready to operate delivers no value.

Projects exist to produce a change that is worth more than it costs. The reason for starting one is written in a business case, which describes the problem or opportunity, the expected benefits, and the money and time the organization is willing to spend. The project charter then formally authorizes the project, names the project manager, and gives that person the authority to use people and money. Without a charter the project manager has responsibility but no stated authority, and every request for resources becomes a negotiation.

Details become clearer as a project moves forward, and plans are refined as more is learned. This is called progressive elaboration. Early estimates are rough, and they tighten as requirements, designs, and risks are understood. Refining the plan is not the same as changing what was agreed: elaboration adds detail inside the approved boundaries, while a change moves the boundaries and needs approval. Related projects are often grouped into a program so that their shared benefits can be managed together, and an organization's programs and projects together form its portfolio, which is chosen to serve its strategy.

## Scope, schedule and cost

Every project balances three competing constraints: scope, schedule, and cost. Scope is the work that must be done and the result that must be delivered. Schedule is the time available to do it. Cost is the budget. The three are linked, so a change to one usually forces a change in at least one of the others. This relationship is often drawn as a triangle with quality in the middle, because quality is what suffers when one side is squeezed and the other two are held fixed.

Consider a sponsor who adds two features without moving the deadline. The scope has grown, so something else has to give: the team needs more money or more people, or a later date, or it will cut corners on testing. A good project manager makes these trade-offs visible and asks the sponsor to choose, instead of quietly absorbing the change. Absorbing it feels helpful in the moment, but it hides the real cost and usually surfaces later as overtime, defects, or a missed date.

The agreed versions of the three constraints are called baselines. The scope baseline, the schedule baseline, and the cost baseline together describe what was approved, and actual performance is measured against them. A baseline is changed only through a formal change request that is reviewed and approved, often by a change control board. Each request is assessed for its effect on all three constraints before a decision is made, so the person approving it knows what the change will really cost.

It helps to know early which constraint matters most to the sponsor. On some projects the date cannot move, such as a product launch tied to a trade show. On others the budget is fixed, or a regulation defines exactly what must be delivered. Asking the sponsor to rank the three constraints at the start gives the project manager a rule for later decisions: protect the constraint that cannot bend, and look for flexibility in the other two. When a change request arrives, this ranking turns a difficult argument into a short conversation.

## Stakeholders

A stakeholder is any person or group who can affect the project or be affected by it. Stakeholders include the sponsor, the team, customers, end users, suppliers, and sometimes regulators or the neighbors of a construction site. Some support the project, some are neutral, and some would prefer that it fail. The project manager identifies stakeholders as early as possible and keeps looking for new ones, because people who are discovered late tend to arrive with requirements that nobody planned for.

Each stakeholder is recorded in a stakeholder register, a document that notes the person's role, interest, influence, and expectations. A common way to sort the register is a grid of power against interest. Stakeholders with high power and high interest are managed closely and consulted often. Those with high power but low interest are kept satisfied, so they do not become obstacles. Those with low power but high interest are kept informed, and those with low power and low interest are simply monitored with minimal effort.

Knowing who the stakeholders are is only the first step; the aim is to bring each one to the level of engagement the project needs. A person can be unaware of the project, resistant to it, neutral, supportive, or actively leading the change. The project manager compares where each key stakeholder is today with where they need to be, and plans actions to close the gap. A resistant department head, for example, might be invited to help define the acceptance criteria, which gives them a stake in the result.

Engagement depends on communication that fits the audience. The communications plan states who needs what information, how often, in what form, and from whom. A sponsor may want a one-page summary every two weeks, while the team needs a short daily conversation. Ignoring a powerful stakeholder until late in the project is one of the most common causes of rework, because their objections arrive after the design is finished. A short conversation early is cheaper than a redesign later.

## Breaking down the scope

Scope work begins with requirements, the conditions the result must meet to satisfy the stakeholders. Requirements are collected through interviews, workshops, observation, and prototypes, and they are written so that each one can be tested. They feed the scope statement, which describes the deliverables, the acceptance criteria, and the exclusions. Exclusions matter as much as inclusions: stating what the project will not deliver prevents arguments later about what everyone assumed.

The work breakdown structure, or WBS, divides the total scope of the project into smaller, manageable pieces. It is a hierarchy: the project at the top, major deliverables below it, and each deliverable divided again until the pieces are small enough to plan. The lowest level of the WBS is called a work package, and it is small enough to be estimated, assigned to one owner, and tracked. The WBS describes deliverables, not activities, so its entries are nouns such as 'training manual' rather than verbs such as 'write the manual'.

A sound WBS follows the 100 percent rule: the pieces at each level add up to exactly the whole of the level above, with nothing missing and nothing extra. Work that is not in the WBS is not part of the project. This rule protects the team from scope creep, the slow growth of scope through small requests that are never formally approved. When a stakeholder asks for something that is not in the WBS, the answer is neither yes nor no; it is a change request, so that the effect on time and cost can be assessed first.

The scope statement, the WBS, and the WBS dictionary, which describes each work package in a few lines, together form the scope baseline. Scope creep should not be confused with gold plating, in which the team adds extras that nobody asked for because they seem like improvements. Both add work without approval, and both spend time and money that the plan assigned elsewhere. At the end, the customer formally accepts each deliverable against its acceptance criteria, which is the proof that the scope has been delivered.

## The critical path

A project schedule turns work packages into activities with durations and dependencies. A dependency says that one activity cannot start until another has finished, just as walls cannot be painted before they are built. Linking the activities in order produces a network diagram, a picture of every route from the start of the project to the end. Each route is called a path, and the length of a path is the sum of the durations of the activities on it.

The critical path is the longest path through the network, and it sets the earliest date the project can finish. Suppose a small project has two routes: design, build, and test take four, ten, and three days, while writing and printing the manual take five and two days. The first route is seventeen days long and the second is seven, so the first is the critical path and the project needs at least seventeen days. Adding people to the manual would not bring the finish date forward by a single day.

Float, also called slack, is the amount of time an activity can be delayed without delaying the project. Activities on the critical path have zero float, which means any delay to them delays the whole project. In the example, the manual has ten days of float, because its route is ten days shorter than the critical one. Float is shared along a path: if writing the manual uses up six of those days, only four remain for printing.

When a project must finish sooner, the project manager looks at critical path activities first, because shortening other activities does not change the finish date. There are two standard techniques. Crashing adds resources to a critical activity to shorten it, which raises cost. Fast tracking performs in parallel activities that were planned in sequence, which raises risk, because some work may have to be redone. After either technique the network is checked again, since shortening one path can make a different path the longest, and the critical path may have moved.

## Identifying risks

A risk is an uncertain event that, if it happens, will affect at least one project objective. The key word is uncertain: a risk lies in the future and may never occur. An issue is different. It is a problem that is already happening and needs action now. 'The supplier might deliver late' is a risk; 'the supplier delivered late yesterday' is an issue. Risks can also be positive, and these are called opportunities, such as the chance that a new tool will cut testing time in half.

Risk identification starts early and is repeated throughout the project, because new risks appear as the work becomes clearer. The team looks for risks in workshops, in interviews with experts, in checklists from earlier projects, and by questioning the assumptions in the plan. A useful risk statement names three things: the cause, the uncertain event, and the effect. 'Because the venue is still being built, the opening may be delayed, which would move the launch past the holiday season' is far more useful than the single word 'venue'.

Each risk is recorded in a risk register with its cause, its possible effect, and an owner, the one person responsible for watching it and for acting if it starts to happen. A risk without an owner is a risk that nobody is watching. The register is a living document. It is reviewed at regular meetings, risks that can no longer happen are closed, and new ones are added, so that it always shows the current picture rather than the one from the planning workshop.

Not every risk deserves the same attention, so each one is rated by probability, how likely it is to happen, and by impact, how much it would hurt or help. Multiplying the two gives a simple score, and a probability and impact matrix sorts the risks into high, medium, and low. The team plans detailed responses for the high risks and keeps the low ones on a watch list. The rating is a judgment, not a measurement, so it works best when several people score each risk and discuss the differences.

## Responding to risks

Once the risks are rated, the team chooses a response for each important one. There are four standard responses to a threat. To avoid a risk is to change the plan so that the risk can no longer happen, for example by dropping an unproven technology in favor of a familiar one. To mitigate is to act now to reduce the probability or the impact, as when a team builds a prototype early to find out whether a design will work.

To transfer a risk is to shift its financial impact to a third party, typically through insurance, a warranty, or a fixed-price contract. Transfer does not make the risk disappear; it gives another party the cost of handling it, usually in return for a fee. To accept a risk is to take no action in advance, either because the risk is small or because any response would cost more than the risk itself. Acceptance can be passive, which means simply dealing with the event if it occurs, or active, which means setting aside time or money in case it does.

Opportunities have matching responses. To exploit an opportunity is to make sure it happens, to enhance it is to raise its probability or its benefit, to share it is to bring in a partner who is better placed to capture it, and to accept it is to take the benefit if it comes without chasing it. For both threats and opportunities, a serious risk can also be escalated when it lies outside the project manager's authority, so that someone with the power to act takes ownership.

A response is a planned action with an owner, and its cost and time belong in the plan. Money set aside for identified risks is called a contingency reserve, and the project manager controls it. A management reserve covers surprises that nobody identified, and using it requires approval from management. A contingency plan describes what to do if a risk occurs, and it is started by a trigger, a warning sign agreed in advance. Responses can create new problems of their own, called secondary risks, and the risk that remains after a response is called residual risk.

## Earned value

Halfway through a project, the sponsor asks a simple question: are we on track? Comparing money spent with money budgeted does not answer it, because spending says nothing about how much work is finished. Earned value management answers the question by measuring three things in the same unit, money. Planned value is the budgeted cost of the work that was scheduled to be done by today. Earned value is the budgeted cost of the work that has really been completed. Actual cost is what that completed work cost in fact.

Take a project to build ten identical kiosks in ten weeks on a budget of 1,000 dollars per kiosk. At the end of week four the plan called for four kiosks, so planned value is 4,000 dollars. Only three are finished, so earned value is 3,000 dollars. The team has spent 3,600 dollars, which is the actual cost. Schedule variance is earned value minus planned value, here minus 1,000 dollars. Cost variance is earned value minus actual cost, here minus 600 dollars. A negative variance is bad news: this project is behind schedule and over budget.

The same numbers can be expressed as ratios, which makes projects of different sizes comparable. The schedule performance index is earned value divided by planned value: 3,000 divided by 4,000 is 0.75, so the team is working at three quarters of the planned pace. The cost performance index is earned value divided by actual cost: 3,000 divided by 3,600 is about 0.83, so each dollar spent buys about 83 cents of planned work. An index below one is unfavorable, an index above one is favorable, and exactly one means the project is on plan.

The indexes also support a forecast. If the team keeps working at the same cost efficiency, the estimate at completion is the total budget divided by the cost performance index: 10,000 divided by 0.83 is about 12,000 dollars. In this example each kiosk is costing 1,200 dollars instead of 1,000, so ten of them will cost 12,000. A forecast like this, made in week four, gives the sponsor time to add money, reduce scope, or change the approach, which is far more useful than discovering the overrun in week ten.

## Agile delivery

A predictive approach, often called waterfall, plans the whole project in detail at the start and then carries out the plan phase by phase. It works well when requirements are stable and well understood, as in building a bridge to an approved design. An agile approach assumes instead that not everything can be known at the start. It delivers work in short iterations, usually one to four weeks long, and each iteration produces a small, working piece of the result that stakeholders can see and use.

Feedback from each iteration shapes what is built next, so the plan changes as the team learns. Agile suits projects where requirements are expected to change as the team learns, such as a new consumer app whose users have not yet said what they want. The length and the cost of an iteration are fixed, and the scope is what flexes: if not everything fits, the least valuable items wait. This reverses the predictive habit of fixing the scope first and then estimating time and cost.

The work to be done is kept in a product backlog, a single list ordered by value. The product owner represents the customer and decides the order, so the most valuable items are always at the top. At the start of each iteration the team pulls from the top of the backlog as much as it believes it can finish. A cross-functional team holds all the skills needed to turn a backlog item into a finished increment, and it organizes its own work instead of waiting for tasks to be assigned.

Short, regular meetings keep the cycle honest. In a brief daily meeting, team members coordinate the day's work and raise anything that blocks them. At the end of the iteration, a review shows the finished work to stakeholders and collects their reactions, and a retrospective lets the team examine its own way of working and choose one or two improvements for the next round. Many organizations combine the two approaches in a hybrid: predictive planning for the parts that are stable, such as hardware or regulatory approval, and agile iterations for the parts that are still being discovered.
