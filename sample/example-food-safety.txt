Food Safety Manager Study Notes

1. The temperature danger zone
Bacteria that cause foodborne illness grow fastest between 41 and 135 degrees Fahrenheit. This range is called the temperature danger zone. Food that needs time and temperature control for safety, often called TCS food, includes meat, poultry, fish, dairy, cooked rice, cut melons and sprouts. TCS food must not stay in the danger zone for more than four hours in total, counting receiving, preparation and service. After four hours it must be thrown away. Cold food is held at 41 degrees or lower and hot food is held at 135 degrees or higher. A manager checks holding temperatures at least every four hours, and every two hours is better because it leaves time for corrective action.

2. Cooking and cooling
Minimum internal cooking temperatures depend on the food. Poultry, stuffed food and reheated leftovers must reach 165 degrees for less than one second. Ground meat must reach 155 degrees for 17 seconds. Whole cuts of beef, pork and fish must reach 145 degrees for 15 seconds. Cooling is done in two stages. Food must cool from 135 to 70 degrees within two hours, and then from 70 to 41 degrees within the next four hours. If the first stage takes longer than two hours, the food must be reheated to 165 degrees and cooled again, or thrown away. Shallow pans, ice baths and ice paddles speed up cooling.

3. Cross-contamination
Cross-contamination happens when pathogens move from one surface or food to another. Raw meat must be stored below ready-to-eat food in the cooler. The storage order from top to bottom is ready-to-eat food, seafood, whole cuts of beef and pork, ground meat, and poultry at the bottom, because poultry has the highest cooking temperature. Separate cutting boards and utensils are used for raw and ready-to-eat food. Surfaces are cleaned and then sanitized after each task and at least every four hours of continuous use.

4. Personal hygiene
Handwashing takes at least 20 seconds, with 10 to 15 seconds of scrubbing with soap. Staff wash hands before starting work, after using the restroom, after touching raw meat, and after handling money or garbage. Single-use gloves are changed when they are dirty or torn, before a new task, and after four hours of continuous use. A food handler with vomiting, diarrhea or jaundice must be excluded from the operation. A handler with a sore throat and fever is restricted from working with food.

5. Allergens
The major food allergens include milk, eggs, fish, shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soy and sesame. Servers must be able to tell guests which dishes contain them. In the kitchen, an allergen order is prepared with clean and sanitized equipment and delivered separately. Cross-contact is when an allergen is transferred to a food that should not contain it, and cooking does not remove it.
